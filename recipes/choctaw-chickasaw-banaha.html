Choctaw-Chickasaw Banaha Bread

Corn Bread / Side · 1 hr 10 min · 6 banaha

Cornmeal, hot water, salt and soda become a compact boiled bread when the dough is wrapped tightly in corn husks and cooked directly in simmering water.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

The Chickasaw Nation publishes this dish specifically as Choctaw-Chickasaw Banaha Bread. Its formula is notably spare: cornmeal, hot water, baking soda and salt are formed into oblong portions, wrapped in softened corn shucks and boiled. The Nation also documents a version with cooked black-eyed peas or red beans. This page stays close to that public community recipe instead of treating banaha as a generic tamale or inventing additional seasonings.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

Banaha belongs to living Choctaw and Chickasaw foodways centered on corn. The Chickasaw Nation preserves the recipe in its cultural foods collection, while Smithsonian programming has described banaha as a traditional Choctaw cornmeal dish and used it to introduce visitors to Choctaw food culture. The wrapper is functional as well as traditional: softened husks hold the stiff cornmeal mixture together while it cooks in water. Leftover banaha can be sliced and fried, a serving method the Chickasaw Nation also records.

This is a home rendering of a recipe the Chickasaw Nation has made public. Keep the Choctaw-Chickasaw attribution attached to the dish. The bean variation below is documented by the Nation, but it is optional rather than a required defining ingredient.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Soften the husks first

Simmer or soak dried corn husks in very hot water about 10 minutes so they bend without cracking; keep them submerged with a plate if necessary.

Aim for a stiff dough

Add the hot water gradually. The mixture should hold an oblong shape in your hand rather than slump like cornbread batter.

Wrap securely

Use two overlapping husks for small or torn wrappers and tie the center with a thin strip of husk or kitchen-safe cotton twine so water cannot wash the dough apart.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 2 cups plain cornmeal
  • 1 tsp baking soda
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1 1/2 cups hot water, approximately
  • 6 to 12 dried corn husks, softened in hot water
  • 1/2 cup cooked black-eyed peas or red beans, drained well, optional documented variation

Method

  1. 1.Bring a deep pot of water to a boil. In a separate heatproof bowl cover the dried corn husks with very hot water for about 10 minutes, until supple enough to fold without splitting; drain and pat them lightly dry.
  2. 2.Whisk cornmeal, baking soda and salt together. Stir in about 1 1/4 cups of the hot water, then add the remaining water a little at a time until a stiff, evenly hydrated dough forms. If using the documented bean variation, fold in the drained cooked beans now.
  3. 3.Checkpoint: squeeze a handful. It should compact cleanly and hold its shape with no dry cornmeal pockets, but it should not ooze or spread. If crumbly, add hot water 1 tablespoon at a time; if loose, let it stand 3 minutes so the cornmeal can absorb moisture.
  4. 4.Divide into 6 portions and shape each into a small oblong roughly the size of a tennis ball. Place each portion on a softened husk, fold the long sides over the dough, fold the ends inward and tie the packet around the middle. Double-wrap any packet whose husk has holes or tears.
  5. 5.Lower the packets into the boiling water, cover the pot and adjust the heat to maintain a steady but not violent boil. Cook 40 minutes, keeping the packets submerged as much as practical.
  6. 6.Lift out one packet with tongs and rest it 3 minutes before opening carefully; trapped steam will be hot. The cornmeal should be fully set from edge to center, moist but not pasty, and the packet should smell distinctly of cooked corn rather than raw meal.
  7. 7.Drain the remaining banaha and serve warm. For leftovers, cool promptly, refrigerate, then slice about 1/2 inch thick and pan-fry until the cut faces are crisp and hot through, following the serving option documented by the Chickasaw Nation.
