Minangkabau Beef Rendang

Slow-Cooked Beef / Main · 7 hrs · 6 servings

Rendang is not rushed: coconut milk first becomes a curry, then an oily kalio, and finally a dark coating that clings to tender beef after hours of patient cooking.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

Rendang is a defining Minangkabau preparation from West Sumatra. Indonesia's national tourism authority explains that the word is tied to the Minang idea of merendang, the slow process that drives liquid from coconut milk while concentrating spice around the meat. Beef rendang daging is the best-known form, but regional rendang can also use other meats, fish, eggs or plant ingredients. This recipe stays specifically with a West Sumatran beef version and follows the staged cooking logic described by Indonesia Travel.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

For Minangkabau communities, rendang is much more than a generic coconut curry. Indonesia Travel documents its place at weddings, religious celebrations and community gatherings and connects its slow preparation with cultural ideas of kinship, gratitude, respect and patience. Another official Indonesia Travel account describes three practical stages: gulai while the coconut milk is still liquid, kalio after it thickens and releases oil, and rendang after prolonged low cooking makes the mixture dark and nearly dry. Keeping those stages visible prevents the common shortcut of stopping at a saucy coconut beef curry and calling it finished rendang.

Rendang varies across Minangkabau communities and beyond West Sumatra. This recipe is a home adaptation of beef rendang daging, not a claim that every family uses the same chile quantity or aromatics. Turmeric leaf is preferred; Indonesia Travel's published William Wongso recipe documents Indonesian bay leaf as an alternative, which is a more culturally grounded substitution than ordinary Western bay leaf.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Use full-fat coconut milk

Rendang depends on coconut fat separating during long cooking. Low-fat coconut milk tends to become thin and can scorch before the sauce develops its characteristic oil.

Expect three stages

Liquid gulai becomes thick, glossy kalio before it becomes dry rendang. The last hour needs frequent stirring because the coconut solids are frying in their own oil.

Keep the heat patient

A hard boil can toughen the beef and scorch coconut solids. Maintain a lazy simmer and increase stirring as the pot gets drier.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 2 lb beef chuck, cut into 2-inch pieces
  • 5 cups full-fat unsweetened coconut milk
  • 2 lemongrass stalks, bruised and tied in knots
  • 6 makrut lime leaves, torn
  • 1 fresh turmeric leaf, tied in a knot, or 2 Indonesian bay leaves (daun salam) as the documented alternative
  • 1 tbsp tamarind concentrate
  • 1 1/2 tsp kosher salt, plus more to taste
  • 10 small shallots, roughly chopped
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 6 to 8 long red chiles, seeded partly if a gentler heat is needed
  • 2-inch piece fresh ginger, peeled
  • 3-inch piece fresh galangal, peeled and sliced
  • 5 candlenuts (kemiri), lightly toasted

Method

  1. 1.Blend the shallots, garlic, red chiles, ginger, galangal and candlenuts with a few spoonfuls of coconut milk into a fine, damp spice paste. Stop and scrape the blender as needed; the finished paste should smell sharply of galangal and chile with no large nut pieces.
  2. 2.Put the spice paste, remaining coconut milk, lemongrass, lime leaves, turmeric leaf or daun salam, tamarind and salt in a wide heavy pot. Bring slowly to a simmer over medium heat, stirring often so the coconut milk does not catch on the bottom.
  3. 3.Add the beef and reduce the heat to maintain a gentle simmer, roughly 190 to 205°F at the liquid. Cook uncovered 2 to 3 hours, turning the beef occasionally. Checkpoint - gulai stage: the pot is still visibly liquid and orange-brown, the beef is becoming tender, and the aromatics smell integrated rather than raw.
  4. 4.Continue cooking 1 1/2 to 2 hours as the liquid reduces, stirring every 10 to 15 minutes. Checkpoint - kalio stage: the sauce should be thick enough to cling heavily to the beef and small pools of reddish coconut oil should appear around the edges.
  5. 5.Lower the heat further once the sauce is very thick. Cook 1 to 2 hours more, stirring and scraping every few minutes and turning the beef gently so the coconut solids brown rather than burn.
  6. 6.Checkpoint - rendang stage: the sauce should be almost dry, dark brown rather than orange, and the separated coconut oil should fry the spice coating with a toasted, nutty aroma. The beef should yield easily to a fork but still hold its shape. If you smell acrid smoke, move the pot off the heat briefly and lower the burner before continuing.
  7. 7.Remove lemongrass and whole leaves. Taste the coating for salt and tamarind. Let the rendang rest 15 minutes; the surface will become slightly less oily as the meat and spice paste settle.
  8. 8.Serve with steamed rice. For storage, cool promptly and refrigerate; the flavor deepens overnight, but reheat gently so the concentrated coconut solids do not scorch.
