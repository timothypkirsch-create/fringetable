Eritrean Taita Fit-Fit (Injera Fit-Fit)

Breakfast / Bread · 30 min · 4 servings

Yesterday's injera becomes a fast Eritrean breakfast when it is torn into pieces and folded through fragrant spiced butter, onion, tomato and berbere.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

Fit-fit is shared across Eritrean and Ethiopian foodways, with names and bread choices varying by language and household. In Tigrinya, injera is commonly called taita, so taita fit-fit refers specifically to the injera-based form rather than kitcha fit-fit made from an unleavened pan bread. The basic structure is torn injera, seasoned fat and berbere; onions, tomato, yogurt, green chile or leftover stew may be added according to the kitchen. This version keeps the dish clearly Eritrean and uses prepared injera so the recipe focuses on the fit-fit technique rather than duplicating a full fermentation project.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

The dish is a practical example of how Eritrean kitchens reuse bread without treating leftovers as second-rate food. Injera's sourness and porous surface make it especially good at absorbing tesmi and chile seasoning. Wikimedia Commons' long-standing photograph identifies taita fit-fit as a traditional Eritrean food, while Eritrean-focused home-cooking sources document the same torn-bread, berbere and clarified-butter logic. Because closely related fit-fit is also eaten in Ethiopia, this page does not claim an exclusive national origin; it preserves the Eritrean Tigrinya name and preparation context instead.

Use actual injera for taita fit-fit. A wheat tortilla, pita or ordinary pancake will absorb sauce differently and should not be presented as equivalent. Eritrean tesmi is preferred; plain ghee is a practical substitute but lacks the aromatic seasoning of tesmi. If your berbere is unusually hot, start with half the amount and add more after the bread is coated.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Use day-old injera if possible

Slightly drier injera absorbs the sauce without collapsing as quickly as very fresh, wet bread.

Tear, do not mince

Pieces about 1 1/2 inches across give a mix of saucy edges and soft centers instead of turning the pan into paste.

Keep the sauce loose before adding bread

Injera absorbs liquid quickly. The onion-tomato base should look glossy and lightly saucy before the bread goes in.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 2 large prepared injera or taita, preferably 1 day old, torn into 1 1/2-inch pieces
  • 3 tbsp Eritrean tesmi or niter kibbeh
  • 1 small red onion, finely diced
  • 1 medium ripe tomato, finely diced
  • 1 to 1 1/2 tbsp berbere spice blend, to taste
  • 1/4 cup hot water or light unsalted broth, plus more only if needed
  • 1/2 tsp kosher salt, plus more to taste
  • Plain yogurt, optional for serving
  • 1 fresh jalapeño or other mild green chile, sliced, optional for serving

Method

  1. 1.Tear the injera into irregular 1 1/2-inch pieces and spread them on a tray while you prepare the sauce. If the bread feels very wet and fresh, let it air-dry for 10 minutes so the pieces hold their shape better.
  2. 2.Melt the tesmi in a wide skillet over medium heat. Add the onion and cook 5 to 6 minutes, stirring, until translucent with lightly golden edges rather than deeply browned.
  3. 3.Add the tomato and cook 3 to 4 minutes until it softens and releases juice. Stir in the berbere and salt for 20 to 30 seconds, just until the fat turns brick-red and smells strongly aromatic; do not scorch the chile spices.
  4. 4.Pour in 1/4 cup hot water and simmer 1 minute. Checkpoint: the pan should contain a loose, glossy red sauce that coats a spoon but still flows when the skillet is tilted.
  5. 5.Add the torn injera in two batches, folding gently with a broad spatula after each addition so the sauce reaches most surfaces without crushing the bread.
  6. 6.Cook 2 to 3 minutes over medium-low heat. Checkpoint: the injera should be hot all the way through, stained red-orange and soft, but many pieces should remain visibly distinct rather than dissolving into a uniform mash.
  7. 7.If dry patches remain, sprinkle in hot water 1 tablespoon at a time and fold once or twice. If the fit-fit looks soupy, leave it uncovered for 30 to 60 seconds; injera will continue absorbing liquid as it sits.
  8. 8.Taste for salt and berbere, then serve immediately. Offer plain yogurt and sliced green chile separately so diners can adjust richness and heat without changing the base preparation.
