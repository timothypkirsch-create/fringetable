Chickasaw Pashofa

Corn and Pork Stew / Main · 5 hrs 30 min · 8 servings

Pashofa is deliberately simple: cracked corn, pork, water and patient simmering become a substantial Chickasaw community food whose character comes from the corn rather than a long seasoning list.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

The Chickasaw Nation preserves pashofa in its public cultural-food materials as a traditional preparation centered on specially processed cracked corn cooked with pork. The defining ingredient is pashofa corn rather than canned supermarket hominy, and the cooking is long and gentle so the kernels soften without dissolving. This home version follows that restrained structure and avoids layering in aromatics that are not part of the documented Chickasaw formula.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

Pashofa remains strongly associated with Chickasaw community gatherings and cultural continuity. Chickasaw Nation food materials present it alongside other living traditional foods rather than as a museum reconstruction, and public demonstrations emphasize the labor of preparing and tending the corn. Related corn-and-pork dishes are known among neighboring Southeastern Native communities, but this page keeps the framing specifically Chickasaw and does not treat one community's method as a generic Indigenous recipe.

Use food-ready dried cracked white hominy or corn specifically sold for pashofa. Traditional corn processing belongs to community knowledge and should not be improvised with unknown ash or lye concentrations. Canned whole hominy shortens cooking dramatically but produces a different texture and is not presented here as an equivalent substitute.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Buy the right corn

Look for dried cracked white hominy or corn labeled for pashofa. The pieces should be irregular and dry, not canned whole kernels.

Plan for a long simmer

The corn needs hours of gentle cooking. Add hot water as needed rather than raising the heat to force tenderness.

Season late

Pork can contribute substantial salt, especially if using neck bones or another cured/salted cut. Taste the finished broth before adding much extra salt.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 1 lb dried cracked white hominy or pashofa corn, rinsed and soaked 8 hours or overnight
  • 2 lb pork shoulder, cut into 2-inch pieces, or meaty fresh pork neck bones
  • 12 cups water, plus more hot water as needed
  • 1 tsp kosher salt, only as needed after cooking
  • Freshly ground black pepper, optional at the table

Method

  1. 1.Drain the soaked cracked corn and place it in a large heavy pot with 10 cups of the water. Bring to a boil, skim any loose foam, then immediately lower the heat to a gentle simmer.
  2. 2.Cook the corn uncovered or partially covered for 2 hours, stirring from the bottom every 15 to 20 minutes. Add hot water whenever the kernels are no longer freely moving in broth.
  3. 3.Checkpoint: after about 2 hours the corn pieces should be visibly swollen and beginning to split at their edges, but the centers will still feel firm when bitten. The broth should smell distinctly of cooked corn rather than raw meal.
  4. 4.Add the pork and enough hot water to keep everything covered by about 1 inch. Return to a very gentle simmer; avoid a hard boil, which can shred the pork before the corn is tender.
  5. 5.Continue simmering 2 to 3 hours, stirring more frequently as the stew thickens. If using pork shoulder, it should become fork-tender; if using neck bones, the meat should pull easily from the bones.
  6. 6.Checkpoint: the pashofa is ready when the cracked corn is fully tender through the center with a soft, pleasantly chewy texture, the broth is cloudy and lightly thickened by corn starch, and the pork has no rubbery connective tissue. Pork should reach at least 145°F, though long simmering will usually take it well beyond that point into tender braising temperatures.
  7. 7.Remove any bones and loose cartilage. Taste the broth before adding salt, then season only as much as needed. If the pot is thicker than a loose stew, stir in a little hot water rather than letting it become a stiff porridge.
  8. 8.Rest 10 minutes off the heat and serve hot. Keep the presentation simple so the flavor of the corn and pork remains central.
