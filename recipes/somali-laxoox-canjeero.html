Somali communities / Horn of Africa

Somali Laxoox (Canjeero)

Fermented Flatbread / Breakfast · 10 hrs 30 min · 8 flatbreads

A mildly sour batter cooks only from below and by trapped steam, creating a browned underside and the soft, porous top that Somali cooks recognize by its many eyes.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

A 2022 in-field study of 46 Somali women documents the flatbread as laxoox in northern regions and canjeero in southern Somalia. Nearly all respondents used wheat flour, while many combined wheat with sorghum, maize, barley or other grains. Fermentation, a medium-hot griddle, one-sided cooking and a hole-filled surface are central characteristics. This practical home formula uses wheat plus sorghum and a very small amount of commercial yeast to make the process reproducible; the research makes clear that household fermentation systems and grain blends are far more varied.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

The Journal of Ethnic Foods survey traces laxoox/canjeero through Somali household knowledge rather than a single standardized recipe. Historically sorghum and maize were important, while refined wheat became increasingly common. Fermented batter is commonly held overnight, and the bread is overwhelmingly associated with breakfast. Respondents paired it with sweet Somali tea, oil and sugar, suqaar, stews, liver, eggs, ghee and other foods. The study also distinguishes heritage cajiin methods and reserved fermented starter, or dhanaanis, from newer and diaspora adaptations.

Commercial yeast is a practical home-kitchen aid, not the default traditional ferment: the field study found respondents generally relied on wild fermentation or reserved fermented batter and did not regularly use commercial yeast. If you maintain an active laxoox/canjeero starter from a trusted household method, use that instead and adjust fermentation time by smell, bubbles and experience.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Ferment by signs, not only the clock

The batter is ready when bubbles are visible across the surface and it smells pleasantly fermented and lightly sour. A cool kitchen may require longer than 10 hours.

Keep the batter pourable

After fermentation it should flow from a ladle in a thick ribbon and spread with a circular motion, not sit like pancake dough.

Cook one side only

A fitting lid traps steam so the top cooks without flipping; the many open holes and a dry, set surface are the visual checkpoint.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 cup fine sorghum flour
  • 1/2 tsp instant yeast, practical home adaptation
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 2 1/4 cups room-temperature water, plus more after fermentation as needed
  • Neutral oil, for a very light film on the griddle
  • Sesame or other vegetable oil and sugar, optional for a documented breakfast serving style
  • Sweet Somali tea, eggs, suqaar or stew, optional accompaniments

Method

  1. 1.Whisk the all-purpose flour, sorghum flour, yeast and salt in a large bowl. Add 2 1/4 cups water and whisk vigorously for 2 minutes, scraping the sides, until no dry pockets remain and the batter is smooth enough to pour.
  2. 2.Cover loosely and leave at room temperature overnight, about 8 to 12 hours. Do not seal the container airtight.
  3. 3.Checkpoint before cooking: the surface should show obvious bubbles and the batter should smell mildly sour and fermented, not putrid or sharply unpleasant. If it shows no activity in a cool room, give it more time in a warmer location rather than increasing heat aggressively.
  4. 4.Whisk the fermented batter for 30 seconds. Add water 1 tablespoon at a time until it pours in a thick continuous ribbon; it should be thinner than American pancake batter but not watery.
  5. 5.Warm a flat cast-iron or nonstick griddle over medium heat. Wipe it with the thinnest film of oil. Pour about 1/3 cup batter near the center and use the ladle bottom to guide it outward in a spiral into a thin 7- to 8-inch round.
  6. 6.Cover immediately and cook 2 to 4 minutes without flipping. Keep the heat medium: the bread should steam and develop holes steadily rather than scorch before the top sets.
  7. 7.Checkpoint: the upper surface should be matte, soft and pockmarked with many open 'eyes,' with no wet batter remaining; the underside should be lightly to moderately browned and briefly crisp at first, while the interior stays soft and chewy.
  8. 8.Slide onto a plate and repeat, adjusting the heat if the bottoms darken before the tops cook. Serve warm for breakfast with sweet Somali tea and, according to preference, oil with sugar or savory accompaniments such as eggs, suqaar or stew.
