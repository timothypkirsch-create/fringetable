Bengali Shorshe Ilish (Hilsa in Mustard Sauce)

Mustard Hilsa Curry / Main · 1 hr · 4 servings

Shorshe ilish is an exercise in restraint: oily hilsa, fresh mustard, green chile and turmeric need little else, but the mustard must be ground cool and the fragile fish barely handled.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

Shorshe ilish means mustard hilsa. The dish belongs across Bengal, with particular prominence in Bangladesh, where ilish is the national fish and an important source of food, livelihood and cultural memory. Bone-in hilsa steaks are coated with turmeric and salt, then cooked gently in fresh mustard paste, green chile and mustard oil. The sauce should remain bright, pungent and fluid enough to run through plain rice. This version does not fry the steaks first, preserving the especially soft texture and allowing the fish's own abundant oil to enrich the mustard gravy.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

Hilsa migrates between the Bay of Bengal and the region's rivers, linking the dish to monsoon seasons, fishing communities and the ecology of the Padma, Meghna, Ganges and Hooghly systems. Bangladesh's Department of Fisheries identifies ilish as the national fish and manages sanctuaries, juvenile-catch restrictions and seasonal protections, while WorldFish describes it as present in the country's markets, poetry, history and dinner tables. Shorshe ilish is one celebrated expression of that relationship, but not the only one: hilsa is fried, steamed, wrapped in leaves and cooked with several regional sauces. Mustard-ilish preparations also vary between Bangladeshi and Indian Bengali households in seed ratios, frying, poppy seed, yogurt and the thickness of the gravy.

Hilsa contains many fine forked bones and must be eaten slowly and attentively; warn every diner and do not serve bone-in pieces to young children or anyone unable to manage them safely. Buy mature, legally harvested hilsa from a reputable source and respect current local fishery restrictions—juvenile jatka should not be purchased. Use only mustard oil explicitly sold for culinary use in your jurisdiction, never a cosmetic or external-use product. Mackerel is an oily practical substitute, but the result is mustard fish, not shorshe ilish, and should be named accordingly.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Grind mustard cold and briefly

Soak the seeds, use cool water and pulse rather than running the blender continuously. Excess heat and damaged hulls can make the paste harshly bitter.

Choose a wide pan

All hilsa steaks should fit in one layer. Stacking or repeated turning breaks the delicate fish and makes the fine bones harder to navigate.

Source with care

Frozen mature hilsa from a reputable Bengali or Bangladeshi grocer is preferable to illegally caught juvenile fish. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator and pat dry gently.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb bone-in hilsa steaks, about 6 pieces, thawed if frozen
  • 1 tsp ground turmeric, divided
  • 1 1/2 tsp fine sea salt, divided, plus more to taste
  • 3 tbsp yellow mustard seeds
  • 1 tbsp black or brown mustard seeds
  • 5 fresh green chiles, divided
  • 5 tbsp culinary mustard oil, divided
  • 1/2 tsp nigella seeds, optional
  • 3/4 cup warm water, plus cool water for soaking and grinding
  • Steamed short- or medium-grain white rice, for serving

Method

  1. 1.Put the yellow and black mustard seeds in a small bowl, cover with cool water and soak 30 minutes. Drain thoroughly. Keep the water and blender jar cool rather than warm so friction does not turn the paste bitter.
  2. 2.Pat the hilsa steaks dry without pulling at the flesh. Rub them gently with 1/2 teaspoon turmeric and 1/2 teaspoon salt and set aside for 15 minutes while making the paste.
  3. 3.Blend the drained mustard seeds with 2 green chiles, 1/2 teaspoon salt and 3 tablespoons cool water in short pulses. Scrape the jar and add water 1 teaspoon at a time until the paste is smooth and pourable, never letting the jar become hot.
  4. 4.Taste only a tiny dab of the mustard paste. It should be powerfully pungent but not acrid. For an especially smooth sauce, push it through a fine sieve and discard the coarse hulls rather than continuing to grind them.
  5. 5.Heat 4 tablespoons culinary mustard oil in a wide lidded skillet over medium until shimmering. If using nigella, add it and cook 20 to 30 seconds, just until fragrant; do not let the seeds blacken.
  6. 6.Lower the heat. Stir in the mustard paste, remaining 1/2 teaspoon turmeric and 3/4 cup warm water. Add 1/2 teaspoon salt and 2 slit green chiles, then bring only to a gentle simmer for 3 minutes. The raw edge should soften while the sauce stays yellow and fluid.
  7. 7.Slide the hilsa steaks into the sauce in one layer and spoon gravy over their tops. Cover and cook over low heat for 5 minutes, keeping the sauce at a lazy simmer rather than a hard boil.
  8. 8.Turn each steak once with a thin spatula and support it with a spoon. Cover and cook 4 to 6 minutes more. Checkpoint: the flesh should be opaque and moist, separate at the outer edge under gentle pressure and register 145°F at the thickest part.
  9. 9.Remove the pan from the heat. Add the remaining green chile, slit lengthwise, and drizzle over the final 1 tablespoon culinary mustard oil. Cover and rest 5 minutes so the sharp aroma settles into the sauce without further cooking the fish.
  10. 10.Taste the gravy around the fish and adjust salt with a light hand. Serve one steak per diner with plenty of hot rice and explain the fine bones before anyone begins eating.
  11. 11.Cool leftovers within 2 hours and refrigerate in a shallow covered container for no more than 1 day. Reheat once, covered and very gently, adding a spoonful of water so the sauce loosens without breaking the fish.
