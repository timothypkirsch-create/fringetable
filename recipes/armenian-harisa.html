Armenian Harisa (Wheat and Chicken Porridge)

Slow-Cooked Wheat and Chicken Porridge / Main · 5 hrs 30 min, plus overnight soaking · 6 servings

The transformation is the recipe: patient simmering bursts the grain and loosens the meat, while vigorous beating turns two humble ingredients into one elastic, deeply comforting dish.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

Armenian harisa—also spelled harissa or herisa—is a thick porridge of hulled wheat and meat, usually chicken or lamb. It is unrelated to the North African chile paste that shares an English spelling. Korkot or dzavar, wheat with the tough outer hull removed, is the defining grain; pearled wheat sold for harees is a practical equivalent. Once the wheat has fully burst and the chicken has become tender, the mixture is beaten until the grain and meat no longer read as separate components. Melted butter, often with cumin, completes each bowl.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

Harisa is everyday nourishment with ceremonial and communal weight. Armenian accounts connect large cauldrons of the dish to religious observances, charity meals and the annual commemoration of the 1915 defense of Musa Dagh. That history should not be collapsed into a tidy origin legend: wheat-and-meat porridges have long circulated across Western Asia, and Armenian harisa is one culturally specific member of that wider family. What distinguishes the Armenian practice is not only its ingredients but the hours of shared cooking and the final act of beating the pot to a homogeneous texture.

Use hulled or pearled whole wheat labeled korkot, dzavar or harees wheat—not bulgur, cracked wheat or ordinary hard wheat berries with intact hulls. Pearl barley softens similarly but changes the grain and should be disclosed as a substitution. The dish is sometimes made with lamb and household proportions vary. This chicken version is a manageable home pot, not a recreation of a commemorative communal cauldron. When discussing Musa Dagh, treat the connection as living Armenian memory rather than culinary spectacle.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Soak the correct wheat

An overnight soak shortens the simmer, but the grain must still cook until every kernel has burst before beating begins.

Use a heavy pot

Harisa becomes extremely thick and scorches easily. A wide enameled Dutch oven and the lowest stable flame give the safest margin.

Beat while hot

A sturdy wooden spoon works best. The finished mass should turn glossy and lift in soft strands rather than remain a bowl of distinct grains and shredded meat.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 2 cups hulled or pearled wheat, sold as korkot, dzavar or harees wheat, about 400 g
  • 1 whole chicken, 3 to 3 1/2 lb
  • 10 cups water, plus more hot water as needed
  • 2 1/2 tsp kosher salt, divided, plus more to taste
  • 6 tbsp unsalted butter
  • 1 1/2 tsp ground cumin
  • 1/2 tsp freshly ground black pepper
  • Pickled vegetables and lavash, for serving, optional

Method

  1. 1.Rinse the wheat until the water runs nearly clear, cover it by at least 3 inches with cool water and soak 8 to 12 hours. Drain and rinse once more before cooking.
  2. 2.Put the chicken in a heavy 7- to 8-quart pot with 10 cups water and 1 teaspoon salt. Bring slowly to a boil, skim the gray foam, then lower to a gentle simmer and cook 60 to 75 minutes, until the thigh meat pulls easily from the bone.
  3. 3.Lift the chicken to a tray. Strain the broth into a heatproof bowl, wipe the pot clean and measure 8 cups broth back into it, supplementing with hot water if necessary. Keep any extra broth hot for loosening the porridge.
  4. 4.When the chicken is cool enough to handle, remove and discard the skin, bones and cartilage. Shred the meat very finely, feeling carefully for small bones, and set it aside.
  5. 5.Add the drained wheat to the broth and bring to a boil. Reduce to the lowest steady simmer, cover with the lid slightly ajar and cook 2 1/2 to 3 1/2 hours. Stir and scrape the bottom every 20 minutes after the first hour, adding hot broth or water whenever the wheat threatens to sit dry on the pot.
  6. 6.Test several grains against the side of the pot. Continue cooking until every one smears easily and most kernels have burst beyond recognition; a chewy center means the wheat needs more time and liquid.
  7. 7.Stir in the shredded chicken and 1 teaspoon salt. Simmer uncovered 30 minutes, stirring every few minutes, until the spoon leaves a path that closes slowly and no loose broth pools at the edge.
  8. 8.Lower the heat to its minimum. Beat the porridge firmly against the side of the pot with a sturdy wooden spoon for 15 to 25 minutes. It is ready when the meat fibers disappear into the wheat, the surface turns glossy and a lifted spoon pulls a soft ribbon rather than dropping separate grains.
  9. 9.Taste and add the remaining 1/2 teaspoon salt if needed. If the harisa stands in a rigid mound, beat in hot water a tablespoon at a time; it should be thick and cohesive but still spoonable. Keep it covered over very low heat.
  10. 10.Melt the butter in a small pan until foaming. Stir in the cumin and black pepper for 15 seconds, without burning them. Spoon the hot harisa into warm bowls, make a shallow well in each serving and pour the fragrant butter into it.
  11. 11.Serve immediately with pickles and lavash if desired. Cool leftovers in shallow containers within 2 hours and refrigerate up to 4 days; reheat gently with water or broth, stirring until the porridge is steaming and smooth again.
