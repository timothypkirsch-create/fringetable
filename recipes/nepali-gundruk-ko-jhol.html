Nepal / Himalayas

Nepali Gundruk ko Jhol

Soup / Side · 40 min · 4 servings

Fermented dried greens give this Nepali broth its unmistakable sour, earthy depth, balanced by tomato, garlic and a gently spiced tempering.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

Gundruk is made by fermenting leafy greens—often mustard, radish or cauliflower leaves—and then drying them for storage. Simmered as gundruk ko jhol, those greens become a tart, earthy broth commonly served with rice or dhido.

Packaged gundruk varies dramatically in salt, sourness and toughness. A quick rinse removes dust, while soaking softens very dry leaves and gives the cook a chance to taste before seasoning the pot.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

Fermentation and drying turn a seasonal crop into a pantry food that can carry leafy-green flavor through colder or leaner months. Gundruk is therefore more than an unusual souring ingredient: it reflects household preservation knowledge and the agricultural rhythms of Nepal's hills.

“Jhol” describes a liquid preparation, and the desired result is brothy rather than a dry sauté. Tomato softens the fermentation's sharper edge; garlic, ginger and cumin build warmth without covering it.

Household versions may include potato, soybean, timur, fresh chile or no tomato at all. Adjust the soak and simmer to the particular gundruk you have.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Taste the gundruk

Its salt and acidity determine how much seasoning the broth needs.

Keep the soaking liquid optional

Use it only if clean and pleasantly sour, never if gritty or harsh.

Preserve the tang

Boil long enough to tenderize the greens, but season at the end.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 2 ounces dried gundruk
  • 2 cups warm water, for soaking
  • 2 tablespoons mustard oil or neutral oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1 small onion, thinly sliced
  • 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • 1 teaspoon grated fresh ginger
  • 1 green chile, slit, optional
  • 2 medium tomatoes, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 4 cups water
  • 3/4 teaspoon fine salt, plus more to taste
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Cooked rice or dhido, for serving

Method

  1. 1.Pick through the gundruk and discard any grit or unusually hard stems. Rinse quickly under cool water.
  2. 2.Cover with 2 cups warm water and soak 15–20 minutes. Drain, squeeze gently and chop long pieces; reserve the soaking liquid only if it tastes clean and pleasantly tart.
  3. 3.Heat the oil in a saucepan over medium heat. Add cumin seeds and let them sizzle for about 20 seconds without blackening.
  4. 4.Add onion and cook 5–7 minutes until soft and lightly golden. Stir in garlic, ginger and optional chile for 30 seconds.
  5. 5.Add tomato and turmeric. Cook 5 minutes, crushing the tomato, until it becomes saucy and the oil begins to show at the edges.
  6. 6.Stir in the soaked gundruk and cook 2 minutes so the leaves take on the aromatic base.
  7. 7.Add 4 cups water, or replace up to 1 cup with clean soaking liquid for a sharper broth. Bring to a boil, then simmer partially covered for 20–30 minutes.
  8. 8.Checkpoint: the greens should be tender enough to bite through and the broth distinctly sour but not abrasive. Very tough gundruk may need longer.
  9. 9.Season with salt only after tasting. Add hot water if the broth has reduced too far, or simmer uncovered briefly if it tastes weak.
  10. 10.Stir in cilantro and rest 5 minutes. Serve hot with rice or dhido, offering fresh chile separately.
