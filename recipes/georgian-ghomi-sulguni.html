Samegrelo / Western Georgia / Caucasus

Georgian Ghomi with Sulguni

Corn Porridge / Side · 1 hr · 6 servings

In western Georgia, ghomi is a soft, elastic corn staple made for scooping up strongly flavored dishes; pushing sulguni into the hot porridge adds salty pockets of melting cheese.

Fringe Table adaptation

About this dish

Ghomi is especially associated with Samegrelo in western Georgia, where it functions less like a standalone polenta course and more like a staple beside stews, beans, sauces and cheese. Modern ghomi is usually corn-based and can combine coarse meal with a finer flour added near the end to create its characteristic thick, cohesive texture. Sulguni is commonly served with or tucked into hot ghomi, where the cheese softens without disappearing completely.

Story & history

Why this dish matters

Georgia Travel presents ghomi as a hallmark of western Georgian, particularly Mingrelian, cooking. The dish's grain history is older than modern maize cultivation: Georgian accounts connect the name ghomi with an earlier millet-like grain before corn became dominant after its arrival in the region. That change is a useful reminder that traditional foodways can absorb new crops over centuries without becoming culturally generic. Today's corn ghomi remains closely tied to Samegrelo and to the region's preference for bold accompaniments such as sulguni, lobio and spicy sauces.

This recipe follows the contemporary corn-based Mingrelian style. Italian polenta can be cooked to a similar consistency, but a strongly yellow, quick-cooking polenta changes both the look and grain character; coarse white cornmeal plus fine white corn flour is a closer home-kitchen fit. Sulguni is traditional but may be omitted rather than replaced with a strongly flavored unrelated cheese.

Before you begin

Preparation notes

Use two grinds of corn

Coarse meal provides structure while a smaller amount of fine corn flour added near the end makes the porridge smoother and more cohesive.

Stir from the bottom

Ghomi gets very thick. A sturdy wooden spoon and frequent bottom-scraping prevent a scorched layer from flavoring the whole pot.

Serve immediately

Ghomi firms quickly as it cools. The best texture is hot, glossy and soft enough for sulguni to melt where it touches the porridge.

Cook it

Ingredients & detailed method

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups coarse white cornmeal, preferably stone-ground
  • 6 cups water, plus more hot water as needed
  • 1 tsp kosher salt
  • 1/2 cup fine white corn flour
  • 8 oz Georgian sulguni cheese, cut into thick strips or bite-size pieces, for serving

Method

  1. 1.Bring 6 cups water to a boil in a heavy pot. Whisk in the coarse white cornmeal gradually, then reduce the heat to low as soon as the mixture returns to a simmer.
  2. 2.Cook 30 to 35 minutes, stirring every few minutes with a sturdy wooden spoon and scraping the bottom and corners. Add a splash of hot water whenever the porridge becomes too stiff to stir before the grain is tender.
  3. 3.Checkpoint: the coarse grains should taste fully hydrated with no hard gritty center, and the pot should smell sweet and cooked-corn-like rather than raw or scorched.
  4. 4.Sprinkle in the fine corn flour a few tablespoons at a time while stirring continuously so it disappears into the hot porridge without forming dry pockets.
  5. 5.Continue cooking 10 to 15 minutes over very low heat, beating and folding the ghomi with the spoon. Checkpoint: it should become thick, glossy and cohesive, pulling away from the pot in heavy folds while remaining soft enough to spoon rather than slice.
  6. 6.Season with salt. If the ghomi is stiff enough to stand in a dry mound, beat in hot water 2 tablespoons at a time until it relaxes to a soft, elastic consistency.
  7. 7.Spoon the ghomi into warm bowls or onto a serving platter. Push pieces of sulguni partly into the hot porridge and leave them 1 to 2 minutes so the edges soften and become stretchy while the centers retain some body.
  8. 8.Serve immediately with Georgian beans, stews or vegetable dishes. Ghomi should be treated as the starch foundation of the meal rather than buried under sweet toppings.
